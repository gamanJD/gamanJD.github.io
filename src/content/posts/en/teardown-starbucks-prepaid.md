In my last post I laid out three structures that keep customers: break-even feel, how often gains and losses come to mind, and loyalty that continues because cutting ties feels too costly. This time I'm doing a deep dive into one company: Starbucks Korea, the brand that has scaled "pay first, drink later" further than anyone else in the country.

Starbucks Korea is operated separately from Starbucks' US headquarters. Today it's run under Shinsegae, one of Korea's major retail groups, and it has its own app, payment rules, and rewards program. Many US readers know Mobile Order & Pay. Fewer know it started in Korea.

Start with one number. According to Starbucks Korea's audited financial statements, customers had about ₩427.6 billion (roughly $300 million) sitting in prepaid balances at the end of 2025. That's coffee money paid for and not yet drunk.

Why do customers pay before they even order? And when does that structure backfire?

## 1. Three mechanisms

| Mechanism | What it is | Key number |
|---|---|---|
| Starbucks Card (stored value) | Load money in the app or on a physical card, pay with the balance | ~₩427.6B outstanding, end of 2025 |
| Siren Order | Order and pay in the app before you arrive | Launched May 2014; 700M+ cumulative orders (Jan 2026); used by ~40% of customers |
| Starbucks Rewards (stars) | Earn stars on purchases and redeem them for perks | June 2025 revamp: higher earning threshold, choose-your-reward redemption |

*Based on public reports and company announcements through October 2026. USD figures are approximate.*

These aren't three separate features. They form one loop: load money → order in the app → earn stars. Let's run that loop through the three structures.

## 2. The teardown

### (1) Break-even feel: how stars changed the sense of getting your money's worth

For years, Starbucks Korea's stars were simple. Collect a set number and a free-drink coupon appeared automatically. The math was easy: a few more drinks, one free.

On June 17, 2025, the program changed significantly.

1. The minimum purchase needed to earn a star went up.
2. Instead of an automatic free-drink coupon, members now choose how to redeem stars, from size upgrades to food and merchandise.
3. Members at the entry-level Green tier became eligible to redeem stars too.

The company said more people now benefit, and reported that star-earning events among Green members more than doubled year over year. Regular customers, though, complained that stars got harder to earn.

The split reaction makes sense. Customers judge a program by how much they feel they're getting back, not by its total value. "My stars used to add up quickly and now they don't" lands harder than "you have more options." When you redesign benefits, the company's math and the customer's math are not the same.

### (2) Felt often: Siren Order sells time, not discounts

Siren Order launched in Korea in May 2014. Starbucks Korea calls it the world's first mobile ordering service of its kind, and the model later spread to other markets. As of January 2026 it had passed 700 million cumulative orders, and about 40% of customers use it, higher than the Asia-Pacific average. Usage grew across every age group from 2021 to 2025. Roughly half of customers in their 50s use it.

Siren Order's benefit isn't money. It's not waiting in line. And customers feel it every single visit, which is exactly the "felt more often than billed" test from my previous post.

Stored value removes one more step. With a Starbucks Card registered in the app, the order is paid from the balance without choosing a payment method each time. Prepaid works less as a discount and more as a way to remove friction.

### (3) Cost of leaving: the leftover balance brings you back

The strongest effect of stored value is simple. If there's money left on the card, you come back. You think about trying the café next door, then remember the balance. Money already spent decides the next visit.

Refund rules reinforce this. Following a standard contract template from Korea's Fair Trade Commission for stored-value gift products, Starbucks Korea's card terms allow a cash refund of the remaining balance only after at least 60% of the last loaded amount has been used (as of the time of the controversy below). The rule has a legal basis. To customers it still means: money you load here gets spent here.

The company benefits too. Cash arrives before revenue does, and it can be invested. Citing Financial Supervisory Service data submitted to Korea's National Assembly in 2025, Korean media reported that Starbucks Korea earned about ₩40.8 billion (~$29 million) in interest income on prepaid balances since 2020, mostly from deposits and trusts.

## 3. The dark side: the May 2026 refund rush

How strong the structure was became clearest when it cracked.

In May 2026, Starbucks Korea was hit by a major brand controversy. The company apologized. Boycott calls followed, along with a wave of customers asking for their prepaid money back.

That's when the 60% rule became a problem. For customers who wanted to leave, "spend 60% more before we refund the rest" felt less like loyalty and more like a hostage situation. Critics also pointed out that the ₩400-billion-plus balance sits outside financial regulators' supervision, because a closed-loop card usable only at the issuer isn't classified as an electronic payment instrument under Korean law. The earlier reports of interest income resurfaced.

Starbucks Korea ultimately waived the 60% condition and offered full refunds for two weeks, June 1 to 14. According to company data obtained by a member of the National Assembly's Political Affairs Committee, about ₩76 billion (~$55 million) was refunded in that window, 17.8% of the end-of-2025 balance.

The lesson for anyone designing loyalty:

> Customers who stay because it's convenient and customers who stay because they can't easily leave look identical, until trust breaks.

Many people counted as "loyal" may have been staying for the balance. They were also the first and loudest to push back.

## 4. Takeaways for planners

1. Design prepaid to remove payment friction, such as hassle and burden, not as a discount. Starbucks Korea's stored value works because it's fused with mobile ordering. If you add prepaid, start by answering: what hassle does it take away?
2. Separate "stay because it's easy" from "stay because it's hard to leave." Convenience-based lock-in survives a crisis. Cost-based lock-in, like balances and refund conditions, turns against you when trust breaks. The wider you open the exit, the more comfortable customers feel staying.
3. Test benefit changes against the customer's sense of break-even, not your own math. Even if total value goes up, a program reads as a cut if customers feel they're getting less back.

Starbucks Korea is the clearest example of prepaid plus mobile ordering done right, and of where loyalty built on money stops working. Whether it's a membership or stored value, customers should stay not because they can't leave, but because they have no reason to.
