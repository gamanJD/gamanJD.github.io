When you design a membership program, the first question is almost always: *what benefits should we include?* How big a discount, how many coupons, which partner perks.

The more I look at memberships that work, the more I think the structure of the benefits matters more than their size. Customers aren't buying a discount. They're buying a reason to keep paying every month.

Korea is an unusually good place to see this. Picture Amazon Prime, Walmart+, and DashPass competing for the same household in one compact, mobile-first market. Here, those players are:

- Coupang WOW: Coupang is often called "the Amazon of Korea." WOW bundles free fast delivery and returns, free food delivery through Coupang Eats, and a streaming service, Coupang Play.
- Naver Plus: Naver is Korea's dominant search portal and a major shopping platform. Its membership boosts cashback points on purchases and includes one digital content pick per month, with Netflix's ad-supported plan among the options.
- Baemin Club: Baemin is Korea's largest food delivery app. Its membership centers on free delivery, and an October 2026 revamp added points on spending.

## 1. The three memberships at a glance

| | Coupang WOW | Naver Plus | Baemin Club |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly fee | ₩7,890 (~$5.70) | ₩4,900 (~$3.50) | ₩3,990 at launch (~$2.90) |
| Core value | Free delivery and returns, food delivery, streaming | Shopping cashback, content pick incl. Netflix | Free food delivery, grocery discounts, points |
| How often you feel it | Every order | Every purchase, plus monthly content | Every delivery order |

*Prices and benefits reflect public information as of October 2026 and may change. USD figures are approximate.*

On the surface it looks like a "who gives more" race. Underneath, each one holds on to customers in a different way.

## 2. Three structures that keep customers

Memberships that work have answers ready for three questions customers ask right before they hit cancel.

### (1) Churn vs. break-even: do customers know their own break-even?

When someone considers canceling, they run a quick calculation: *did I get my money's worth this month?*

Naver Plus makes that math effortless. Points show up as a number after every purchase, and at roughly ₩100,000 (~$70) of monthly shopping the cashback already exceeds the fee. Customers can see they've "broken even."

Coupang WOW works differently. Shipping fees you didn't pay are invisible, so Coupang frames the value against what you *would have paid*: about ₩3,000 per delivery, ₩5,000 per return. A single free return nearly covers the month.

The lesson for planners:

> Customers stay as long as they feel they're getting more than they paid.

And that feeling comes less from a company claim like "members save $X on average" than from one number customers can see on their own receipt.

### (2) Felt often: do people feel the benefit more often than they pay for it?

Billing happens once a month. How many times a month does the customer feel *this membership just paid off*?

This is where Baemin Club shines. Food delivery is a weekly habit for many people, and every order shows a ₩0 delivery fee. One charge a month, several moments of felt value.

A generous benefit that's used rarely is weak by comparison. A birthday coupon once a year leaves a monthly question behind: *I didn't use it this month, did I?*

So I look at a simple ratio first:

- Moments of felt value ÷ billing events. Is it comfortably above 1?
- Is the benefit attached to the customer's most frequent action: ordering, paying, visiting?

Baemin's October 2026 move to add points on spending reads the same way. It layers a growing number (1) on top of a frequent benefit (2).

### (3) Cost of leaving: what do you lose when you cancel?

The third structure is switching cost. Does canceling cost you one thing, or several at once?

Cancel Coupang WOW and you lose free shipping, free returns, free food delivery, and streaming, all at the same time. People who joined for shopping get hooked on Eats or Coupang Play, and leaving gets harder.

Naver Plus strengthened this structure by adding Netflix's ad-supported plan as a pick. That plan costs more on its own than the whole membership, so canceling means paying Netflix separately. Family sharing adds to it: cancel, and your family loses benefits too.

> The real job of a bundle isn't to add value. It's to raise the cost of leaving.

## 3. The three structures side by side

| | Break-even feel | Felt often | Cost of leaving |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coupang WOW | ○ vs. shipping and return fees | ◎ every order | ◎ shopping + food + streaming |
| Naver Plus | ◎ visible points | ○ at purchase | ◎ Netflix + family sharing |
| Baemin Club | ○ ₩0 delivery fee | ◎ weekly orders | △ delivery-centric |

Each has a clear strength and a clear homework assignment. Baemin Club is strong on frequency but lighter on switching cost, which helps explain why it keeps adding things to bundle: grocery discounts, points.

For US readers the pattern will feel familiar. Prime layered Video and Music on top of shipping. Walmart+ added Paramount+. DashPass partnered with credit cards. Korea just runs the experiment faster and in a denser market.

## 4. Five questions before you pick a discount rate

1. Do customers know their break-even? Can you show "you saved X this month" in the app?
2. Is the benefit felt more often than it's billed? Is it tied to the most frequent action?
3. What do customers lose by canceling? One thing, or several?
4. Can a non-heavy user break even? If the break-even point is too high, people never sign up.
5. What happens when you need to cut benefits? Memberships built on discount wars lose trust the moment benefits shrink.

The last one matters most to me. Memberships that compete on the *size* of benefits eventually hit a profitability wall. Memberships that compete on *structure* keep customers longer for the same cost.

A membership isn't a discount product. It's a design that makes customers conclude, every single month, that there's still a good reason to stay.
